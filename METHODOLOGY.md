# Methodology

This file says how every label, count and estimate in the essay is made. The reasons for the choices are in [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md); what the results cannot support is in [LIMITATIONS.md](LIMITATIONS.md). Numbers quoted here are as of the data snapshot and are regenerated by `python run_all.py`.

## 1. Unit and snapshot

The unit is a CVE record. The vulnerability records were downloaded from the National Vulnerability Database on November 10, 2026, and the study covers records published from January 1, 2019 to November 9, 2026, the last full day. The 12 records dated November 10 are in the data file and skipped when it is loaded. Every time axis is the date the vulnerability was **published**, not the date it was attacked: Chart 2's 2025 bars count vulnerabilities published in 2025 that have been attacked at any time since.

## 2. Which vulnerabilities are counted

**The reference class** (every row in `data/flaws.csv.gz`, 78,992 records) is a CVE that meets either test:

- *Remote code execution* (`rce`): the NVD description says remote code or command execution, or says code or commands can be executed and the CVSS vector is network or adjacent; or the weakness is command injection, code injection, deserialization or dangerous file upload with full confidentiality and integrity impact; or it is a remotely reachable memory-corruption write scored as full compromise. Code execution when a user opens attacker-supplied content (a local vector in CVSS terms) is included, because vendors and attackers both treat it as remote. Cross-site scripting and plain SQL injection are excluded from this test. The rule is in `pipeline/upstream/rcedef.py`; the `basis` column records which branch fired.
- *Remote and high impact* (`pop2`): the CVSS vector is network, and confidentiality, integrity and availability impact are all High. This brings in login bypasses, SQL injection and similar vulnerabilities that give an attacker full control without being labeled code execution.

Charts 3 and 5 call the reference class "remotely exploitable vulnerabilities".

**Delivery** (`delivery`) comes from the CVSS vector: `user` if a user must do something, `creds` if the attacker needs a login, `open` if neither is needed and the vector is network or adjacent. Charts 1, 2 and 4 use only `open`: no login and no click.

**The class** (`cls()` in `src/study.py`; Charts 1 and 2) is the most dangerous subset: `open` delivery, in internet-facing enterprise software (`role` is `edge`, such as firewalls and VPN gateways, or `srv_exposed`, such as mail, file-transfer and web application servers). 4,916 records since 2019.

## 3. Labels on each vulnerability

All labels are assigned by the same rules to every record, without looking at whether the vulnerability was exploited.

- **Product role** (`role`): a vendor table, then bounded keyword rules on product names and descriptions (`pipeline/upstream/roles2.py`). Values: `edge`, `srv_exposed`, `srv_internal`, `client_os` (PCs, phones, browsers and their apps), `iot`, `ics`, `cloud`, `component` (libraries), `plugin`, `hobby` (student and demonstration projects), `other`, `other_web`. The last two mean the rules could not place the product.
- **Weakness** (`wk`): from CWE numbers, then from the description: memory corruption, authentication, SQL injection, command injection, upload, other injection, deserialization, other.
- **Who installs the fix** (`upd`): `vendor` (installed by the vendor by default, or the vendor runs the service), `optional` (offered automatically but someone must accept it), `customer` (the customer downloads and installs it), `downstream` (a library: every product that embeds it must ship its own fix). Set from a researched table of 62 product families with dates (`pipeline/inputs/update_models_major_products.csv`), otherwise from the product role (`pipeline/upstream/update.py`). `upd_basis` says which.
- **Maker group** (`group`), used in Charts 3, 4 and 5:

| Group | Chart label | Who |
|---|---|---|
| `hyperscale` | Hyperscalers | Google, Apple, Meta, Amazon (`company` says which) |
| `microsoft` | Microsoft | Microsoft and its subsidiaries |
| `large` | Large vendors | Commercial vendors with revenue of $5bn or more |
| `midmarket` | Mid-market and smaller vendors | Commercial makers of enterprise and industrial software with revenue under $5bn, including about 1,800 vendor names with no revenue on record whose products are servers, edge devices, client software, industrial systems or cloud services |
| `oss` | Open-source projects | Foundation and community projects, libraries, and records issued by open-source numbering authorities |
| `small` | Plug-ins and small projects | WordPress and other plug-ins, student and hobby code, and a catch-all: software whose kind of maker the rules could not classify |
| `device` | Device makers | Makers of routers, cameras and other small devices |

  Revenue comes from an 86-vendor panel built from filings (`data/vendor_panel.csv`) and a researched extension table. Where no revenue is known the group follows the product role (`pipeline/upstream/vendattr.py`). `group_basis` records the route: `revenue`, `named`, `rule` or `override`. Corrections made by hand, 47 vendor names in all, are in `data/group_overrides.csv` with a reason for each.

## 4. Exploitation evidence

**Attacked** (`attacked()`): the vulnerability is in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, or appears in any of the researched lists of exploited vulnerabilities (`pipeline/inputs/exploitation_evidence_lists.csv`: 1,937 entries with source, list name and URL), or meets the mass-exploitation test below. 977 records.

**Mass exploitation** (`mass()`): public evidence of attacks at scale. A vulnerability counts if any of these holds:

1. It is known to be exploited and has at least one strong kind of evidence: CISA's ransomware flag, a ransomware advisory, botnet use, a government list of the most-exploited vulnerabilities, a sensor or telemetry ranking, Rapid7's "widespread" classification, or a named campaign with a scale figure.
2. It appears in one of CIRCL's quarterly lists of the 100 most-sighted exploited vulnerabilities (`data/sensor_lists.csv`, quarters from October 2024), and the product is not client software.
3. It was used in a named campaign with a published victim count (`data/campaigns.csv`).

465 records qualify. Left out on purpose: evidence that only says a spyware vendor or an intelligence service used the vulnerability; zero-day reviews that give no scale; the July–September 2024 sighting list, which predates the Shadowserver honeypot feed and includes entries with a single sighting; all-time sighting lists; and sensor rankings for client software, because the sensors are honeypots that see attacks on servers. **Some exploitation** in Chart 2 means attacked but not mass-exploited.

**Zero-day** (`zd`): a researched source says attackers were using the vulnerability before a fix existed. 240 records.

## 5. The 2026 estimates (Charts 1 and 2)

Evidence of exploitation arrives after publication, so raw counts for recent quarters are too low. `src/estimates.py` corrects for this in three steps.

**Publications in October–December.** The quarter was 40 days old at the snapshot. Its bar in Chart 1 is October's count times three (247 × 3 = 741).

**Vulnerabilities already published.** For each quarter the observed count is divided by the share of the evidence expected to have arrived:

- *Attacked*: the share comes from the delay between publication and listing in CISA's catalog, measured on vulnerabilities of the class published since 2022 (207 of them). Because recent vulnerabilities have had less time to be listed, the delay curve is estimated with a product-limit estimator for right-truncated data. It says 63% of eventual listings arrive within 30 days, 78% within 90, 86% within 180 and 98% within a year.
- *Mass-exploited*: the share comes from the number of quarters between publication and first appearance in a quarterly sensor list, measured on 54 vulnerabilities published since October 2024, with the same correction: half appear in the quarter of publication, 72% by the next, 84% within three.

**The rest of October–December.** Attacked vulnerabilities are assumed to keep arriving at the July–September pace for the 52 days left: the quarter's estimate is the lag-adjusted count for what is already published plus 52/92 of the July–September estimate.

**Ranges.** 4,000 bootstrap draws resample both delay samples, redraw the observed 2026 counts from a gamma distribution (count + ½), and widen October–December by the error the full-quarter rule showed in back-testing. Reported ranges are the 5th to 95th percentiles.

**Back-tests.** The rules were run as of day 40 of each of the 13 quarters from July 2023 to September 2026 and compared with what CISA has listed since. Only CISA listings carry dates, so the tests cover "attacked" and publications, not mass exploitation.

| Rule | Estimate | Outcome | Typical miss in one quarter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Publications: first month × 3 | 2,565 | 2,529 | 10% (worst: 19% low, 49% high) |
| Delay adjustment, current quarter (12 cut-offs) | 73 | 64 (raw count at the time: 38) | |
| Delay adjustment, previous quarter | 124 | 118 (raw count at the time: 92) | |
| Full-quarter rule, as used | 167 | 165 | 19% (worst: 37% low, 84% high) |
| Full-quarter rule, earlier draft (previous quarter's rate per vulnerability) | 193 | 165 | 29% |

**Results.** Of the class published in 2026: 108.5 attacked, which the post rounds to about 110 (range 89 to 148), against 47 in 2025, and about 38 mass-exploited (26 to 64) against 23. October–December: about 40 attacked, of which 10 are observed.

## 7. Application-security budgets (Chart 6)

No company publishes this figure. `src/budgets.py` estimates it two ways and combines them, treating every input as a 90% range of a lognormal quantity:

- **A.** Engineers × the share working on application security (1% to 4%) × fully loaded cost per head ($280,000 to $570,000) × overhead for tools, compute and bounties (1.1 to 1.6).
- **B.** Software-development spending × the share spent on application security (0.6% to 3.5%).

The two are pooled in log space weighted by precision, and the pooled spread is widened by half because both routes lean on the same judgment about the share. Company inputs with their sources are in `data/budget_inputs.csv`; the judgments and their basis are in `data/budget_assumptions.csv`. The result for each company is a middle estimate with a 90% range of about a factor of 2.6 either way. Mid-market revenue is the latest fiscal year for the 39 vendors under $5bn in the panel.

## 8. Vendor attributes (the "I also tested…" sentence)

`src/maturity.py` takes the class, in products customers patch, published 2019–2025, from vendors in the panel: 1,081 vulnerabilities, 48 vendors, 107 mass-exploited. For each of nine attributes it compares the share mass-exploited between vendors with and without it. Vulnerabilities cluster within vendors, so the chance figure comes from shuffling the attribute across vendors 4,000 times, not across vulnerabilities.

| Attribute | With | Without | Chance of a gap this large |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public paid bug bounty | 32 of 311 | 46 of 506 | 78% |
| Signed the Secure by Design pledge | 47 of 450 | 59 of 622 | 80% |
| Founded after 1996 (the median) | 32 of 268 | 69 of 781 | 41% |
| Flagship codebase newer than 2002 | 20 of 175 | 66 of 685 | 68% |
| Flagship product acquired, not built | 42 of 401 | 55 of 541 | 94% |
| R&D above 18% of revenue | 33 of 303 | 33 of 492 | 27% |
| More than 5,847 employees | 64 of 824 | 35 of 215 | 2% |
| Revenue above $1.3bn | 65 of 838 | 42 of 243 | 1% |
| Private-equity owned | 36 of 170 | 71 of 911 | 0.3% |

Nine tests were run, so a gap with a chance figure of 1% or 2% is weaker evidence than it looks. Private-equity ownership is the only result that would survive a correction for nine tests.
